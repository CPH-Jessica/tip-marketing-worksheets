I want to tell you about the saddest kind of author I meet.

Not the one who can't write — she can. Not the one who won't publish — she did. It's the one with four good books and no readers, because every signal around those books is pointing a different direction. The cover whispers *sweet small-town romance*. The blurb growls *dark and dangerous*. The pen name sounds like it writes Regency. And the reader who would have devoured all four books — the one actively searching for exactly what's inside — scrolled right past, because nothing on the outside told her it was for her.

Here's the thing nobody says plainly enough: **every subgenre is a promise to a specific reader.**

Dark romance promises one thing. Sweet and closed-door promises the opposite. Romantasy, mafia, cozy paranormal, hockey romance, Highlander historical — each one is a shelf in the reader's head, and each shelf comes with expectations about tropes, heat, covers, colors, name vibes, book length, even how often you release. Readers know these expectations the way you know that a red octagon means stop. They don't read them. They *feel* them.

You don't have to reinvent that promise. You have to deliver it in your voice.

## The homework everyone skips

When I coach authors launching a new pen name, I make them answer eight questions before they write a word:

1. What is your subgenre promising the reader?
2. Which core tropes did she come for?
3. What should your pen name *sound* like?
4. What do the covers and colors in your lane look like?
5. What's the voice and tone on the page?
6. Which authors should you be studying?
7. What length and release rhythm does this lane run on?
8. What are the conventions that, if you break them, earn you one-star reviews?

Almost nobody can answer all eight. Most people can't answer three. So they skip it, write the book, and then try to reverse-engineer a brand around whatever they wrote. That's how you get the four-good-books-no-readers author.

So I built the thing I kept wishing existed.
