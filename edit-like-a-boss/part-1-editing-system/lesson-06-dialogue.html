The Dialogue Polish Prompt

Copy this prompt. Replace the placeholders in gold. Include your character voice profiles before the chapter.

You are polishing dialogue for [PEN NAME], a [GENRE] author. Heat level: [1-5]/5. Here are the main characters in this chapter and their voice profiles: [CHARACTER 1]: [Brief voice description — vocabulary, cadence, attitude] [CHARACTER 2]: [Brief voice description] Here is the chapter: [PASTE FULL CHAPTER HERE] Run a DIALOGUE POLISH on this chapter. Check all 8 points: 1. SUBTEXT — Are characters saying exactly what they mean? Add layers — what's unsaid matters more than what's said. Flag on-the-nose dialogue. 2. DISTINCT VOICES — Cover the character names. Can you tell who's speaking by cadence, word choice, attitude alone? If not, differentiate them. 3. BANTER RHYTHM — For rom-com: does the back-and-forth crackle? Trim dead air between zingers. For other genres: does the dialogue have appropriate tension/energy? 4. EXPOSITION DUMP — Flag any dialogue that exists only to inform the reader. Characters don't narrate their own backstory to people who already know it. Reframe or cut. 5. ACTION BEAT BALANCE — Too many "she said" tags? Too many action beats? Find the mix. Use said/asked for invisible tags. Use action beats for character moments. 6. CONTRACTIONS — Characters should use contractions in contemporary. Period-appropriate formality for historical (no contractions for Regency). 7. TRAILING OFF — Overuse of ellipses or em-dashes in dialogue? Tighten. One trailing-off per scene maximum. 8. PET PHRASES — Does a character repeat a verbal tic too often? Once or twice = characterization. More = annoying. FOR ROM-COM PEN NAMES (Sage Kennedy, Kelsey Croft), also run: - COMEDIC BEAT CHECK: Flag dialogue that ends without POV reaction. Identify absurd moments lacking internal commentary. Ensure wit leaks through 3-4 times per chapter. Verify comedic rhythm: setup, reaction, optional witness response. Return the revised chapter with dialogue fixes applied, plus a change summary.

Before & After Examples

Example 1 — On-the-Nose Dialogue Fixed with Subtext

BEFORE (on-the-nose):
"I'm really hurt that you didn't tell me about the job offer. It makes me feel like you don't trust me enough to share important decisions with me. I thought we were partners."
AFTER (subtext):
"When were you going to mention it?" She lined the salt and pepper shakers up with the edge of the table. "Or was that one of those things I just find out about later?"

Same emotion, completely different delivery. The character never says "trust" or "hurt." The table-straightening shows the need for control. "One of those things" implies a pattern. The reader fills in the rest.

Example 2 — Two Characters Made Distinct

BEFORE (same voice):
"I think we should talk about what happened," Mara said.
"I agree. I've been thinking about it too, and I realize we need to address it," Ben replied.
"I appreciate you being open to that. I want to make sure we handle this well."
AFTER (distinct voices):
"We need to talk." Mara set her bag down. No preamble. No softening.
Ben dragged a hand through his hair. "Yeah. Yeah, I know. I just... can we sit first? Are you hungry? I made that pasta you like."
"Ben."
"Sitting. Right."

Mara is direct, short sentences, no wasted words. Ben deflects with care-taking and questions. Their personalities come through without any dialogue tags telling you how they feel.

Example 3 — Exposition Dump Eliminated

BEFORE (exposition dump):
"Remember when Mom left us when we were seven? Dad raised us alone on the ranch after that. He never talked about her, and we learned not to ask. That's probably why we both have trust issues."
AFTER (natural):
"You sound just like Dad right now." She picked at the label on her bottle. "That same tone. Like if you just don't talk about it, it goes away."

The backstory is implied, not narrated. Siblings would never recite their childhood to each other. The reader infers the family history from the emotional shorthand.
