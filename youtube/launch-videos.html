Title (74 chars)
I Edit My Novels With AI in 9 Passes — Full System (Edit Like a Boss)
Full Script — ~1,250 words ≈ 10–11 min
Cold open — to cameraOne editing pass will never be enough. Not for a human editor, and definitely not for AI. When you ask AI to "edit everything" in one go, it does everything badly — it averages. The fix is nine passes, one job each. This is the exact system I use on every manuscript, start to finish. IntroI'm Jessica Waldron from The Invisible Pen. This system is called Edit Like a Boss, and today you get the whole map — what each pass does, in what order, and why the order matters. The principleThe principle first: one pass, one job. When AI has a single job — find plot holes, nothing else — it's shockingly good. When it has five jobs, it's mediocre at all of them and it starts rewriting your voice while it's at it. Nine focused passes beat one giant one, every time. Here we go. Pass 1 — Quality Check · SCREEN: worksheetPass one: the quality check. Before you fix anything, you diagnose. The AI reads the manuscript and reports — where the pacing sags, which characters blur, what's working. You're not editing yet. You're getting the map so the next eight passes have targets. Pass 2 — Plot holesPass two: plot holes. Continuity, logic, the gun that was never fired, the injury that vanished by Thursday. AI is genuinely better at this than most humans — it doesn't get absorbed in the story, which for once is an advantage. It flags; you decide what's actually a problem. Pass 3 — TimelinePass three: timeline. If your heroine is pregnant for eleven months, this is where you catch it. Days of the week, travel time, healing time, school semesters. Tedious for you, trivial for AI. That's exactly the kind of work you should be delegating. Pass 4 — Line editPass four: the line edit. Sentence-level tightening — filter words, echoes, flabby constructions. This is the pass where your banned words list and style sheet earn their keep, because a line edit without them is how manuscripts get their voice sanded off. Flag mode, always: the AI suggests, you choose. Pass 5 — DialoguePass five: dialogue. Does every character sound distinct? Could you strip the tags and still know who's talking? This pass checks rhythm, slang consistency, and whether your teenager talks like a forty-five-year-old lawyer. Unless both characters are supposed to sound the same — that's a flag you overrule. Pass 6 — Heat scenesPass six: heat scenes — and if you don't write romance, this is your high-stakes-scene pass: fights, reveals, deaths. The scenes readers actually bought the book for get their own dedicated pass. Choreography, emotional continuity, heat level consistent with the promise on the cover. Pass 7 — DevelopmentalPass seven: the developmental edit. Structure. Arcs. Does the midpoint actually turn? Does the black moment land? This is the big-picture pass, and it comes late on purpose — no point checking structure while the timeline's still broken and the scenes are still moving around. Pass 8 — Voice auditPass eight: the voice audit. After seven passes of edits, you check the cumulative drift: does this still sound like ME? The AI compares the edited manuscript against your style sheet and your voice samples and flags every place the edits pulled you toward generic. Anything flagged, you restore by hand. Pass 9 — The human passPass nine. You. Out loud, printed if you can, no AI in the room. You are the last editor, always. This pass is non-negotiable, and it's the reason this system produces books that sound like their authors — because the final judgment on every single line was human. Recap · SCREEN: all 9 passes listedThe whole system on one screen: quality check, plot holes, timeline, line edit, dialogue, heat scenes, developmental, voice audit, human pass. Diagnose first, details in the middle, structure late, voice and human last. CTAEvery pass you just saw has a worksheet with the exact prompts — those come with my class, Edit Like a Boss, linked below. It's self-paced, it covers building your AI editing team and using ChatGPT and Gemini alongside Claude, and it assumes zero technical background. If you're not ready for the class, start with the free editing videos coming on this channel — one deep-dive per pass. Subscribe so you don't miss those. One pass, one job. Go edit like a boss.

Title (67 chars)
Write the Book Readers Are Already Searching For (Trope Stacks)
Full Script — ~1,000 words ≈ 8 min
Cold open — to cameraThere are two kinds of books: the book you wanted to write, and the book readers are searching for. The magic happens when they're the same book — and there's a tool for making that happen BEFORE you write a word. It's called a trope stack. IntroJessica Waldron, The Invisible Pen. Today is marketing you do at the OUTLINE stage — the cheapest, most powerful marketing there is. Author intent vs reader intentFirst, the idea underneath everything: author intent versus reader intent. Author intent is "I want to write a story about grief and second chances set in a lighthouse." Beautiful. Reader intent is what the reader types into Amazon or hears on TikTok: "grumpy sunshine small town romance." Readers don't search for themes. They search for tropes. This is not about writing to trend or abandoning your story. It's about knowing which shelf your book lives on while you can still build the shelf INTO the book. What a trope stack isA trope stack is three to five tropes that define your book's promise. Not one — one trope is a category, not a book. "Enemies to lovers" describes ten thousand books. "Enemies to lovers, forced proximity, one bed, hockey romance" — now I can see the cover, the blurb, and the exact reader who one-clicks it. Building the stack · SCREEN: worksheetHere's how you build one. Layer one: your genre's power tropes — the ones with proven, searchable demand. In romance: grumpy-sunshine, fake dating, second chance, forced proximity. Layer two: your setting or world hook — hockey, small town, dragons, academy. Layer three: your twist — the element that's actually yours, the thing that makes it not-interchangeable. The test for a good stack: can a reader repeat it back after hearing it once? "Fake dating with the dragon king's bodyguard" passes. "A meditation on belonging" does not — that's your theme, and it stays, but it's not the stack. Where the stack goes to workNow — why bother? Because the stack becomes everything downstream. Your outline: every trope carries mandatory scenes readers expect, and now they're planned instead of accidental. Your cover brief. Your blurb's first line. Your keywords. Your ad targeting. Your TikTok hook. One decision at the outline stage, and half your marketing writes itself. And if you use AI to help outline — this is exactly what you feed Claude. "My trope stack is X, Y, Z: generate the obligatory scenes each trope promises and flag which ones my outline is missing." That prompt alone is worth the video. CTAThe Trope Stack & Reader Intent worksheet is free below — it walks you through all three layers and pressure-tests your stack. This is week one of something bigger I'm building called Trope School, and subscribers hear about it first. Next week's video takes your stack and turns it into keywords. If you're in a series, ads on book one are really buying read-through to books two, three, and four.
