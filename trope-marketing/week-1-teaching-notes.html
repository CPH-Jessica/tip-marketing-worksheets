💡 The Big Idea (say this in your own words)

Most new authors think tropes are a craft thing — the ingredients of the story. They are. But tropes are also the single most important marketing tool an indie author has, because tropes are the words readers actually search.

A reader almost never types "a moving story about two people learning to trust again." They type second chance small town romance. That's a trope stack. When your book description, keywords, and ads use the same words the reader is already typing, you stop hoping to be discovered and start lining yourself up directly in front of demand.

The line to land the whole class on

"You're not inventing what readers want. You're naming it — in their words — so they can find you."

🎓 Core Concepts to Teach

1. What a trope really is

A trope is a familiar, repeatable story pattern that readers recognize and seek out on purpose — grumpy/sunshine, enemies-to-lovers, fated mates, fake dating. Tropes are not clichés and they are not a lack of originality. They're a promise. When a reader buys "enemies-to-lovers," they are pre-ordering a specific emotional experience. Your originality lives in how you deliver the promise, not in avoiding it.

2. The trope stack

A single trope is a shelf; a trope stack is your address on that shelf. Most books have 3–5 primary tropes. The combination is what makes a book feel specific: "small town + grumpy/sunshine + single dad" is a very different book from "small town + fake dating + Christmas," even though both are small-town romance.

  • Anchor tropes — high-demand, familiar (this is your visibility).
  • Flavour / edge tropes — more specific, more "you" (this is your differentiation).
  • The pro move: pair a hungry, high-demand trope with one more specific trope so you're both findable and distinct.

3. Reader intent (the marketing heart of the lesson)

Reader intent is what a reader is actually trying to do when they type into a search bar. It's demand you can see. A reader searching "spicy age gap billionaire romance" has already decided what they want to buy — they're just looking for who's selling it. Your job: make sure the words on your product page match the words in that search. Reader intent shows up in three places we'll use today:

  • Amazon / Kindle search & keywords — the buying search bar.
  • BookTok / Instagram hashtags — the discovery search bar.
  • Your blurb's first two lines — where you confirm "yes, this is the book you searched for."

4. Niching down

New authors fear a niche makes their audience smaller. Reframe it: a niche makes your audience reachable. "Romance" is 500,000 competitors. "Grumpy single-dad small-town Christmas romance" is a reader who feels like you wrote the book for them — and far fewer authors to compete with for that exact search. Niching down is how a small author out-markets a big one.

Say this"A niche isn't a smaller room. It's a room where you're the only author standing at the front."

🎤 The Class Script (~60 min)

Green = do, coral = say, purple = ask the room. Timings are a guide, not a cage.

0:00 – 0:05 · Open
Say"Welcome to Week 1. Before you write a single word of your next book, you're going to learn what your reader is already hunting for — and how to line your book up right in front of them. That starts with tropes."
Ask"Quick show of hands — who here has ever searched for a book by typing something like 'enemies to lovers' or 'small town romance'? Keep your hand up if you've ever done it as a reader." (Almost every hand stays up. That's your proof.)
0:05 – 0:15 · The Big Idea
Say"Everything you just admitted to searching — those are tropes. Tropes aren't lazy writing. They're the words readers use to find the exact feeling they want to buy. Today we turn that into your marketing."
DoTeach Core Concepts #1 and #2 (what a trope is, the trope stack). Give one concrete stack from your own catalog as an example so it's real, not abstract.
0:15 – 0:22 · Reader Intent live
DoShare your screen with the Amazon store open. Type a trope phrase into the search bar (e.g. "grumpy sunshine romance") and let the autocomplete suggestions appear.
Say"See these suggestions dropping down? That's not Amazon guessing. That's Amazon telling you what thousands of real readers already typed. This is reader intent — visible demand. We're going to make sure your book speaks in these exact words."
Ask"Call out a trope from your genre and let's watch what autocompletes." (Do 2–3 live. It always lands.)
0:22 – 0:45 · Build the stack (worksheet)
DoDrop the worksheet link in chat. Walk them through it live, step by step (see the Live Demo section below). This is the heart of the class — give it the most time.
Say"Open the worksheet. Set your genre and heat at the top. Now pick 3 to 5 tropes that are truly in your book — watch the market signal beside each one. Aim for one or two 'Growth' or 'Evergreen' tropes plus something more specific."
DoAs they select, the worksheet auto-builds their search phrases, hashtags, and positioning line. Pause and let a couple of authors read theirs aloud.
0:45 – 0:55 · Niche it down
DoTeach Core Concept #4, then have them fill the three text boxes in Step 3 (anchor, edge, reader-in-one-sentence).
Ask"Who's got a positioning line they're willing to share? Read me the 'this book is for the reader who…' sentence." Workshop 2–3 out loud — tighten them together.
0:55 – 1:00 · Close & homework
DoPoint them to the Class Toolkit (Step 4 of the worksheet). Give the homework (see Wrap section). Tease Week 2.
Say"You now have something most authors never get: the exact words your reader is searching. Next week we turn that stack into a blurb and Amazon keywords that convert. Print your worksheet — you'll need it."

🖥 Running the Worksheet Live (step by step)

Have the worksheet open on your shared screen. Narrate as you go so they follow along on their own copies.

  1. Step 1 — Genre & heat. Choose a genre from the dropdown and a heat level. Explain: "Genre is the shelf, heat is a filter readers use, tropes are the address."
  2. Step 2 — Trope bank. Click tropes to select. Point out the coloured Growth / Evergreen / Steady / Niche signals — that's live market context. The tool caps them at 6 and nudges toward 3–5.
  3. Watch the Stack panel appear. As soon as they pick one trope, the panel unfolds with three auto-generated blocks:
    • Reader Intent search lines — paste-ready Amazon/keyword phrases.
    • BookTok/Instagram hashtags & a hook — ready for social.
    • A positioning line — "A [genre] where [trope] meets [trope]…"
  4. Step 3 — Niche it down. They write their anchor, their edge, and their one-sentence reader. This is where the thinking happens — give them a quiet minute.
  5. Step 4 — Class Toolkit. These are the other interactive worksheets that pair with this class — Trope Compatibility Checker, Blurb Builder, Content Calendar, Channel Audit, Name Generator. Tell them the natural next step is the Trope Compatibility Checker to pressure-test their stack.
  6. Print / Save as PDF. Everyone saves their sheet before leaving. They bring it to Week 2.

Tech note: the worksheet is fully self-contained — nothing to install, no login, works in any browser, and prints cleanly. If a student's results don't appear, they simply haven't selected a trope yet.

⚖ Serving Both Levels

🌱 For the beginners in the room Keep it to two tropes: one anchor with a Growth/Evergreen signal + one flavour trope they love writing. Reassure them that focus is not limitation — a clear two-trope book is far easier to sell than a vague five-trope one. Their win today is simply being able to name their book in reader language.
⚡ For the seasoned authors in the room Push them to escape the crowd. Their anchor tropes are proven — the growth comes from stacking a third, rarer trope and leading their ads with it. Send them to the Trope Compatibility Checker to confirm the stack sells together, and challenge them to rewrite an existing backlist blurb using today's search phrases.

If someone says "but I don't want to write to trends"

Great objection — welcome it. Answer: "We're not choosing tropes to chase trends. We're naming the tropes already in your book so readers who want them can find you. You wrote the story you wanted. This is just the label on the jar."

Your quick-reference so you can speak to demand with confidence when a student names their trope. Signals reflect the TIP Trope Glossary.

Rule of thumb to repeat: pair niche tropes with market-strong tropes, and always name the trope — in the blurb, the ads, and the newsletter. Readers search tropes, not vague descriptions.

❓ Questions You'll Get (with answers)

"Isn't using tropes just being unoriginal?"

No — a trope is a promise, not a plot. Two authors can both write "fake dating" and produce completely different books. Originality is in the execution; the trope is how readers find the execution.

"How many tropes should I have?"

Three to five primary tropes for most books. Beginners: start with two. More than five and your positioning gets muddy.

"What if my book has a trope readers are polarized on, like love triangle?"

Know your audience. Divisive tropes (love triangle, cheating, certain dark elements) can be a strong draw for the readers who want them — but signal clearly so you attract fans, not one-star reviews.

"Where exactly do I put these words?"

Blurb (first two lines), Amazon's 7 keyword slots, your also-boughts/ad targeting, and your social hooks. Same words, everywhere the reader looks.

"My trope is niche and low-demand — am I doomed?"

No. Anchor it to a high-demand trope so you show up in busy searches, then let the niche trope be the reason that reader chooses you. Niche + market-strong is the winning stack.
