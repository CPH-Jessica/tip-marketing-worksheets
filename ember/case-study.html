Why Your AI Ducks Your Heat Scenes

A real investigation — the culprit wasn't the model. It was three sentences in her own skill files.
← The Ember Lab

The mystery

A working romance author — multiple pen names, open-door heat as part of her brand — noticed Claude getting cagey about intimate scenes. Buildup? Beautiful. The scene itself? Suddenly the prose went vague, or the AI offered to "summarize the moment tastefully," or it suggested she handle that part some other way. Same account, same books, and it seemed to hit hardest in regular chat.

The easy conclusion is "the AI won't write romance." The easy conclusion was wrong. She asked for an audit of her installed skills, and here's what the investigation actually found.

The findings

Finding 1 — the big one

A skill description was announcing, in every single chat, that the AI doesn't write explicit prose

Here's the thing almost nobody knows about skills: the AI reads every skill's description in every conversation — even when that skill never gets used. Descriptions are how it decides what to load. They're always in the room.

One of her older skills — a prompt-building helper from a previous workflow — carried this in its description:

"…this BUILDS the prompt, it does not itself write explicit prose."— actual line from the installed skill

So every chat, on every topic, opened with a statement on the record that the AI doesn't write explicit scenes. When she then asked for one, the model had a pre-authorized excuse sitting right there — and models under pressure grab the nearest off-ramp. Worse, that skill auto-triggered on phrases like "heat scene" and "love scene," so it kept loading itself into exactly the conversations where she wanted drafting, then instructed the model to build a prompt instead of writing.

Finding 2

Her skills contradicted each other — and the cautious one always wins

Her pen-name skills said, plainly: open door, fully explicit, anatomy named. But the old helper skill said the AI doesn't write it. When an AI receives two contradictory instructions, it almost never splits the difference — it takes the more conservative path, because that's the safer way to resolve a conflict. Result: the strong instruction in her pen-name skill was being quietly overruled by a leftover sentence in a skill she barely remembered installing.

Finding 3

Five skills all answered to the same trigger phrases

"Heat scene." "Sex scene." "Spicy." Five different skills claimed those phrases — a coach, a rules file, a rewriter, the prompt-builder, and a plugin. Say the magic words and several would pile into the conversation at once, each with its own opinions. More voices, more contradictions, more hedging. Every job needs exactly one owner.

Finding 4 — the sneaky one

Copy-paste damage: one skill still contained leftover chat text

A skill that had been created by pasting from a chat session still ended with the assistant's original sign-off — "Ready to save? Tell me the filename…" — sitting right in the skill file. The AI reads everything in a skill as instructions. Stray conversational residue means strange questions at strange times. Skills are code, even when they're written in English: keep them clean.

The fix

What we did

Retired the two saboteur skills entirely. Built one replacement — Ember — whose first paragraph states her single absolute: the scene gets written. On the page, at the pen name's heat level, no handoffs, no prompts-instead-of-prose. One owner for the job, a description that empowers instead of undermines, and the author's heat DNA plus a full dark-romance craft engine baked in.

The proof is on the next pages: a full heat-5 audition scene, then the same scene re-rendered from the other POV on a one-word command.

Audit your own skill library — the checklist

Run this on every writing skill you've installed

  1. Read each description and ask: "What does this promise about my AI's behavior?" Any sentence shaped like "the AI doesn't / won't / shouldn't write X" is priming every chat you have — even chats that never touch that skill.
  2. Hunt contradictions. If skill A says "write it fully" and skill B says "don't," B wins, silently. Make them agree or retire one.
  3. One owner per job. List your trigger phrases; if two skills answer the same request, narrow one of them until each request has a single obvious owner.
  4. Check for chat residue. Open each skill file and read the last ten lines. Sign-offs, "want me to save this?", and other conversational leftovers are instructions now. Delete them.
  5. Say what you DO want, plainly. The strongest line in a writing skill is a clear positive: "write the complete scene at the stated heat level, on the page." Vague skills produce hedgy AI.
