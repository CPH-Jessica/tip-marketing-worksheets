Run the newsletter without living in the inbox

How to put a Grok Bot on draft, calendar, and recap — and keep the Send button in your hand.

Promise: By the end of this lesson you will have one newsletter bot with a standing job, one weekly routine, and a hard rule that it never hits Send.

Who this is for: authors who already have a list (MailerLite, Substack, ConvertKit, Beehiiv). Not for people still deciding whether to have a newsletter.

Meet the crew

Draft

Writes the issue. Holds the papers. Never sends them.

Gate

The lock on Send. Cute. Unmovable. That lock is you.

Monday

The calendar belly. Runs the routine while you’re at the store.

Inbox

One owner. Two bots in one inbox and this face is you by Thursday.

Babe, listen

A newsletter bot is not a tiny employee with a Send key. It’s a draft mule with a calendar.

A chat is a conversation. A bot is a job. If the work should still exist on Thursday — same list, same cadence, same voice — it is a bot job. If you need one subject line punched up, stay in chat.

The setup that doesn’t blow up your list

  1. Make one bot whose only job is the newsletter. Name it something boring. Newsletter. Not Sparkle Mail Fairy.
  2. If you already have an email bot, do not make a second one for this class. Hand it the routines.
  3. Write the standing purpose in one paragraph. Job, voice, what it never does. The never-list includes Send, inventing numbers, and touching money mail.
  4. Connect one inbox or one Substack — to that bot only. Strategy can live on a second bot. The inbox cannot.
  5. Feed it three real issues you already sent. That’s the voice sample. Without those, it will write every other AI newsletter on the internet.
  6. Save two routines: Monday draft. Friday “what happened / what’s next.” Both end with leave it here.
  7. You open the draft. You cut the sentence that sounds like a press release. You hit Send.

If you want the bot to also hunt swaps, that is a different job. Separate it or you will get a draft that thinks a BookFunnel swap is your personal essay.

Standing purpose — paste this, then make it yours

You draft my author newsletter. You never send. You keep my voice, my links, and last week’s issues in memory. You run a Monday draft routine and a Friday recap. You flag anything that looks like a sale, a refund, or a reader in distress — and you stop. You do not invent subscriber counts, review quotes, prices, or dates I did not give you. You do not write swap copy unless I hand you the blurb. You leave the draft here.

Monday routine (save this)

Trigger: every Monday 9am (your timezone). Pull last week’s notes if I left any. Draft this week’s newsletter in my voice: 1 hook, 1 story, 1 teaching point, 1 CTA. Three subject lines. Do not invent a sale, a review count, or a date I didn’t give you. Leave the draft here. Do not send.

Friday routine (save this)

Trigger: every Friday 3pm. Recap only. What shipped. What stalled. One sentence on what next week’s issue should open with. Do not invent open rates. Still no Send.

The human gate is the whole point

Set-and-forget is how a fabricated blurb reaches thousands of people. I have that receipt. The mule drafts. You still hit Send.

What it may invent vs what it may not

Allowed: structure, subject-line options, tightening a paragraph you wrote.

Forbidden: subscriber counts, review quotes, prices, “last issue performed great,” links you didn’t supply, other authors’ blurbs unless you handed them over.

Failure modes

  • Two bots in one inbox.
  • Description stuffed with this week’s issue.
  • Bot “helps” by sending.
  • No voice samples, so you get ChatGPT-smooth.
  • “Run my newsletter” with no cadence and no gate. That’s a wish, not a job.

Homework — done means all five

  1. Bot exists (or existing email bot reused).
  2. Standing purpose pasted. Send is not in the purpose line.
  3. Three sample issues uploaded or pasted.
  4. Monday routine saved.
  5. One draft sitting in the thread. Unsent.

Post in this classroom: bot name (not a screenshot of the whole inbox), the standing-purpose paragraph, and the three subject lines from the first draft.
