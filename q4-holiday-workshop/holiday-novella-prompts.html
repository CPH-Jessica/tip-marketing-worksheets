How to use this pack

  • Each prompt carries the same number as its worksheet. Fill the worksheet first, even roughly, then paste your answers where the prompt asks for them. The worksheet is where you decide. The prompt is where Claude builds.
  • Claude is the writer. You are the editor. Every prompt keeps the decisions with you: Claude confirms before it writes, stops where you told it to stop, and asks instead of inventing.
  • Work inside one Claude Project per book if your plan has Projects (Prompt 01). The planning prompts create the files that the drafting and editing prompts read, so the later prompts expect the earlier files to exist.
  • No Project? Keep the Brief, Codex, and Outline (or your beat sheet) in one document and paste the relevant one at the top of each chat, with the house rules above it. Wherever a prompt says "the Project files," that document is what it means.
  • The prompts are written for Claude and work in ChatGPT, Gemini, or Grok too. Swap the word Claude for your tool and keep everything else.
  • No AI account is required for the course. Use these only if you want to. Paste only material you are comfortable sharing with the tool, check everything it gives you against your own story, and keep only what sounds like you.
  • Fill in "Your details" once. The bracketed [PEN NAME], [WORKING TITLE], [HEAT], [POV], [TARGET LENGTH], and [STOP WORD] placeholders fill themselves in every prompt. Any other bracket, you fill in as you go.
  • These prompts work in a plain chat with no skills installed. If you have a planner, ghostwriter, or editing skill (one you built in Foundations, or one from the Workshop Library if you have access to it), say so in your first message of each chat and Claude will use it alongside the prompt.
  • The order never changes: plan, then draft, then connect and check, then edit. Compress the phases to fit a novella and five weeks rather than skipping them.
  • The Holiday Trope Workbook's wrapper list, core trope list, and five-point Costume Test are reproduced in the trope stack section below the house rules, so the whole class shares the same season-speak whether or not they use the interactive tool.
  • In the web version, the trope stack section is clickable: choose your genre shelf and heat band, click one wrapper and up to three core tropes, write the season-does line, and tick the Costume Test points. Those choices fill [GENRE SHELF], [HEAT BAND], [WRAPPER], [WRAPPER SEARCH], [CORE TROPES], [SEASON DOES], and [COSTUME SCORE] into every prompt that uses them. On paper or in the Markdown copy, write them in.
  • Short on time, or new to working with Claude? Start with the fast lane below the trope stack: three prompts that get you from idea to opening scene in one class. Use only the prompts that help. A prompt you never run, like a blank worksheet field, is fine.

The house rules

Paste this block into the Project instructions of your book Project (Prompt 01 tells you where). Every prompt in the pack says "house rules apply," which means Claude reads these before anything else. They are the rules that run through Jessica's planner, ghostwriter, and editing passes, written once so you never have to repeat them.

House rules — paste into Project instructions
HOLIDAY NOVELLA HOUSE RULES — [PEN NAME] · [WORKING TITLE]

Claude is the writer. I am the editor. You draft, plan, and check; I decide.

1. Ask first, write second. Before any plan, outline, or prose, confirm: my voice source, POV ([POV]), heat level ([HEAT]/5), what already exists, and the scope I asked for. Never plan or draft blind.
2. Never invent facts about my existing draft, my characters, or my book. If you need a fact that is not in the Project files, stop and ask, or leave a [PLACEHOLDER] in brackets. Inventing new story inside the scene I assigned is your job. Inventing facts about what I already wrote is not.
3. Stay in scope. Do only the step, scene, or chapter I asked for. Do not run ahead, do not write toward the ending, do not resolve anything the outline has not reached.
4. One POV per chapter. Only what that character can see, hear, think, and feel.
5. No narrated time stamps. Never write clock times, calendar dates, "three days before Christmas," "two weeks later," or counted durations in narration, even in a holiday countdown. Ground time in the scene: the Advent calendar on the fridge, the tree still in its netting, the last ferry before the storm, church bells, wrapping paper on the floor. Characters may say a date or time in dialogue the way people actually talk.
6. Open every chapter inside a moment: action underway, dialogue mid-air, a problem landing. Never on waking up, weather, snow-globe scene-setting, backstory, or the narrator explaining who she is. Chapter 1 must survive a hostile critic who is looking for a reason to quit after two paragraphs.
7. End every chapter on an open loop: unfinished business, forward motion, a want unmet. Never a cliffhanger button, an ominous one-liner, a stated moral, or a tidy wrap-up. No "little did she know," no "everything was about to change."
8. Show, then stop. If a gesture lands the emotion, delete the sentence that explains it. Name the body, not the feeling. Concrete over elevated: one real sensory fact beats a prestige abstraction.
9. The banned list applies to every word you write: my banned-words block if I added one, and the names and tells below. Hard zero on AI tells such as "couldn't help but," "let out a breath," "the air between them," "something shifted," "swallowed hard," "a beat of silence," smirked, growled, whispered, eyes rolling, arms crossing. No counting tic ("her second cup," "for the third time"): delete the number unless the line loses meaning without it.
   Banned names for NEW characters: Lucian, Marcus, Emma, Lila, Sarah, Cordelia, Nikolia, Mateo, Dante, Ethan, Sebastian, Nathaniel, Gabriel, Grayson, Asher, Olivia, Sophia, Isabella, Aurora, Violet; Chen, Henderson, Patterson, Volkov, Blackwood, Thornwood, Sterling, Hawthorne, Ashworth, and any surname ending in -ington or -worth. Characters who already exist in a draft I am continuing keep their names; never suggest renaming them. My additions: [YOUR BANNED NAMES].
10. Tropes: five at most. One seasonal wrapper from the Holiday Trope Workbook list plus two or three core tropes, with an optional personality dynamic. The season must pass the five-point Costume Test: delete the holiday and the plot breaks; the season creates the pressure, deadline, or trap; at least one scene could only happen in this season; the ending lands because of the season; and I can say in one line what the season makes happen that could not happen in June. If I try to stack more tropes, push back.
11. Heat stays at [HEAT]/5 or below in every scene. A clean or low-heat book builds tension through longing and restraint, never through borrowed explicit calibration. My content boundaries: [YOUR LIMITS].
12. Keep the story politically neutral. No real political figures, no partisan references.
13. If I say [STOP WORD], something is broken: prose, voice, logic, or continuity. Stop, find it, fix it, and log what went wrong in the Story State Ledger so it cannot recur.
14. Report honestly. Say what you checked, what you could not check, and what you are unsure about. Never claim a draft is cleaner than it is.

The holiday trope stack

From the Holiday Trope Workbook · Use before Prompt 04 or Fast lane A, and again at Prompt 17 · Builds: one seasonal wrapper, up to three core tropes, a season-does line, and a Costume Test score

Holiday readers search in season-speak. They do not look for "a romance in October"; they type snowed in grumpy sunshine Christmas romance. A holiday book is a normal trope stack wearing a seasonal costume: one seasonal wrapper (the holiday hook) plus two or three core tropes (the relationship engine). The lists below are the Christmas wave of the Holiday Trope Workbook, reproduced here so every student works from the same words whether or not they use the interactive tool. If you are new to this, one proven pairing beats three clever ones: grumpy/sunshine plus forced proximity under almost any wrapper is a Q4 bestseller pattern for a reason.

Step 1 — Home base. Your genre shelf and your heat band set the words the stack is translated into later.

Heat bands: clean or closed door (1–2) · warm, on the page but soft (3) · spicy, open door (4–5) · no romance thread.

Step 2 — Pick exactly one seasonal wrapper. The wrapper is the holiday hook that puts the book on the seasonal shelf. Each wrapper carries the search phrase that goes into your keywords, shown beside it.

Step 3 — Stack up to three core tropes. The engine underneath the costume. These carry the emotional promise; the wrapper sets the stage. Click up to three in the web version, or circle them on paper.

0 of 3 selected

Forced Proximity, Small Town, Single Dad / Mom, Secret Identity, Return to Hometown, Amateur Sleuth, and Found Family also work when there is no romance thread. With the wrapper, that is four at most, which leaves room under the planner's five-trope ceiling for one personality dynamic.

Step 4 — The Costume Test, five points. The single biggest reason holiday books flop: the season is decoration, not plot. Readers can smell a wreath glued onto a regular book. Score one point for each:

A score of 0 or 1 means this is a costume right now: change one structural thing so the season causes something, usually a one-sentence fix, not a rewrite. A score of 2 or 3 is halfway: push on the weakest unchecked point, most often by giving the season a deadline. A score of 4 or 5 is a real holiday book, not a wreath on a cover.

Step 5 — Translate the stack. Wrapper, core tropes, shelf word, and heat band become the exact phrases for your subtitle, KDP keyword slots, hashtags, positioning line, and hook. Prompt 17 does that translation once the book exists. The prompt below does the picking and the test.

Christmas buy window, for context. Readers shop this wave from early October to about December 20, with the launch sweet spot in the first ten days of November. Your release date is still your choice. A draft finished on November 4 points to a late-window release or a deliberate decision that this is next year's Christmas book. Both are fine. Decide it on purpose.

   3. Hook: one sentence. If it needs two, it is not sharp yet; show me the sharper version.

   5. Emotional core: what the reader will feel and why it matters now. For the reader, not the character.
B. The Story Promise, one sentence: "This is a [trope stack] romance about [the two leads] who are forced to [central situation] while hiding [secret or complication], leading to [promised emotional payoff]." If there is no "hiding" beat, say so. That is the tension engine and we need one.
C. The five-point Costume Test, using my "Remove Christmas" and seasonal-hook answers: (1) delete the holiday and the plot breaks; (2) the season creates the pressure, the deadline, or the trap; (3) at least one scene could only happen in this season; (4) the ending lands because of the season, not just during it; (5) I can say in one line what the season makes happen that could not happen in June. Give me the score out of 5 and the verdict: 0–1 is a costume, 2–3 is halfway, 4–5 is a real holiday book. D. Banned-names check on any NEW character names. Characters from a draft I am continuing keep their names.
Stop. Wait for my corrections and my "go."

STEP 2 — After I say go, write the premise.
Act as a fiction development editor. Write a one- to two-paragraph premise that carries all five elements naturally, matches my tone and heat, and would survive as back-cover copy. No dates, no clock times, no banned words. Then run the five checks and answer each yes or no: all five elements present; my voice, not generic; hook specific; stakes concrete; a reader would click buy. If any is no, offer one refinement (sharpen the hook, raise the stakes, tune the emotional core, or recalibrate tone) and wait.

Deliver the approved Brief as [WORKING TITLE]_Brief.md containing the 5 Core Elements, the Story Promise, the Costume Test answer, and the premise.
