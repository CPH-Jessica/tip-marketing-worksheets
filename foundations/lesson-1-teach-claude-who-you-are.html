The Invisible Pen · Setup Worksheet

Teach Claude Who You Are

Claude gives generic advice to strangers and specific advice to people it knows. This worksheet builds your author profile in five short steps — then hands you one block of text to paste into your Claude settings.

Step 1 of 5

Who I Am

Claude can't see your career. If it doesn't know you're a working indie author, it answers like you're a hobbyist — or worse, like you're querying agents in 2009. Watch the difference one sentence makes:

You ask: "Help me plan my next book release."
Claude, guessing

"Congratulations on finishing your book! First, you'll want to decide between traditional and self-publishing. If traditional, research literary agents and prepare query letters. You might also consider hiring an editor, thinking about cover design, and building an author platform on social media..."

Polite. Generic. Useless — it's explaining publishing to a publisher.

Claude, knowing you

"Since this is book three in your small-town series and your income depends on read-through, let's build the release around your backlist: preorder timed to a promo on book one, ARC team briefed two weeks out, and your newsletter sequence teasing the returning characters your readers already love. Want the week-by-week plan?"

It skipped the beginner lecture and went straight to strategy.

Why it matters: one sentence about who you are deletes an entire category of wrong answers.
Example: "a romance author publishing under multiple pen names."
Solo author? Publishing operation? Freelancer with author work on the side?
Fiction sales, coaching, courses, a day job, a mix — be specific.
Step 2 of 5

How My Business Is Structured

If you write under more than one name, use more than one platform, or work with anyone at all, Claude needs the map. Otherwise every task starts with twenty questions — or a wrong guess.

You ask: "Draft an email to my readers about the new release."
Claude, guessing

"Here's a draft! Subject: Exciting News! 🎉 — Dear Valued Reader, I am thrilled to announce my latest novel is now available..."

Which pen name? Which list? Which voice? It wrote a press release for a stranger.

Claude, knowing you

"This one's going to the Claire Sutton list on MailerLite, so I kept it warm and chatty — like a letter from a friend in Millbrook. Subject line options below, plus a P.S. plugging the series starter for new subscribers."

Right pen name, right platform, right voice — first try.

Why it matters: the map of your business turns "draft an email" from a guessing game into a one-step task.
Pen names, businesses, series, products, services. List them plainly.
First name and role is enough. "Just me" is a perfectly good answer.
Amazon KDP, BookFunnel, MailerLite, Substack, Skool, Canva — whatever's real for you.
Step 3 of 5

How I Work Best With You

Left to its defaults, Claude tends to compliment first, hedge often, and explain at length. Some people love that. If you don't, say so once — here — instead of correcting it in every chat.

You ask: "What do you think of this blurb?"
Claude, guessing

"This is a lovely blurb with real emotional resonance! You clearly have a gift for character. That said, if you wanted to consider a small tweak — and this is entirely optional — you might possibly explore whether the second paragraph could perhaps be slightly tightened..."

Three compliments deep and it still hasn't told you what's wrong.

Claude, knowing you

"The hook is buried. Your best line — 'the last man she should trust is the only one who knows the truth' — is in paragraph three. Lead with it. Also, you name four characters in six sentences; readers can hold two. Fix those and this converts."

Direct, specific, done. Because you told it that's how you take feedback.

Why it matters: feedback style is a setting, not a personality trait Claude has to guess.
Bullet points or prose? Short or thorough? Should Claude ask questions first, or attempt and adjust?
Direct and blunt, or diplomatic? Flag problems, or just fix them?
Filler phrases, over-explaining, specific words, emoji, whatever grates.
Give it permission — even obligation — to disagree. When do you want to be challenged?
Step 4 of 5

What I'm Currently Building

Claude will happily brainstorm forty directions for you. Priorities tell it which three you actually need — and which shiny objects to leave on the shelf.

You ask: "Give me ideas to grow my author income."
Claude, guessing

"Great question! You could start a podcast, launch a Patreon, try TikTok, write in a new genre, create merchandise, offer editing services, teach a course, start a YouTube channel..."

Twelve ideas. Eleven of them are distractions from the book you're supposed to be finishing.

Claude, knowing you

"Your 90-day focus is finishing book three, so the highest-leverage moves are ones that pay off at launch: a preorder push on your existing list, a series-starter promo stack for release week, and a read-through audit of books one and two. You've said audio is not a priority right now, so I'm leaving it off the list."

It filtered its own ideas through your priorities — including the "not right now" list.

Why it matters: "not a priority" is some of the most valuable information you can give an eager assistant.
Active projects and what matters most in the next 90 days.
Anything running in the background.
Things Claude should deprioritize if they come up.
Step 5 of 5

Hard Rules

Preferences are suggestions; hard rules are law. This is where you ban the phrases that make your skin crawl and lock in the format you want when you haven't specified one.

You ask: "Write my newsletter intro."
Claude, guessing

"In today's fast-paced world, readers are looking for stories that let them dive into a cozy escape! Let's unpack what makes this month special... 📚✨"

Three banned phrases and two emoji in one breath.

Claude, knowing you

"October in Millbrook means the maple stand opens and Delia's inn is fully booked — and this month, so is my writing schedule. Here's what's coming, including a sneak peek at chapter one."

No filler, no emoji, straight into your voice — because the rules said so.

Why it matters: a "never" list is the fastest edit you'll ever make — you make it once and it applies forever.
Absolute don'ts: phrases, assumptions, formats, content types.
Non-negotiables Claude must always do in your context.
When you haven't specified: length, structure, tone baseline.
Finished
